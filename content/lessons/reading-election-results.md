Election night can feel confusing. Numbers change, news outlets say different things, and some races are not settled for days.

## What "called" means

When a news organization "calls" a race, it is saying one candidate is almost certain to win based on votes counted and votes still out. The [Associated Press](https://www.ap.org/elections/) is one of the main sources for race calls, and it explains its method online.

A call is not official. Election officials must count every valid ballot, check totals, and then "certify" the results. Certification is the official finish, and the timeline differs by state.

## Why some races take days

Several things slow the count:

- **Close margins.** If candidates are separated by a few hundred votes, outlets wait.
- **Mail ballots.** Some states process these early. Others cannot start until Election Day, so counting takes longer.
- **Late-arriving ballots.** Some states accept mail ballots postmarked by Election Day that arrive later.
- **Provisional ballots.** These need to be checked before they are counted.
- **Rules about verification.** Signature checks and fixing small errors take time.
- **Large or slow-reporting counties.** Totals can arrive in batches.

Early numbers can also be misleading. Some areas report first, and the mix of votes counted so far may not match the final mix. This is sometimes called a "shift" and it does not mean something is wrong. Keep an eye on the percent of votes reported.

## Runoffs

In some states, if no candidate gets the required share in an election, usually a majority, the top two face off again in a runoff. [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/primary-runoffs) lists which states use primary runoffs. Runoffs are held weeks after the first election.

## Recounts

If a race is very close, a recount may happen. Rules differ:

- Some states require an **automatic recount** below a certain margin.
- In others, a candidate can **request** one, sometimes by paying.
- Recounts can be done by machine or by hand.

Recounts usually change totals by only a small number of votes. [NCSL's recount page](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/election-recounts) summarizes each state's recount rules.

## Where to look for official results

Trust official sources. Your state's or county's election website posts results, and [USA.gov](https://www.usa.gov/election-results) points you to your state or local election office. Final numbers are the certified ones.

## Staying calm

If a race is close, patience is normal. A slow count does not by itself mean there is a problem.

## Key takeaways

- A "call" is a news projection. Certification is the official result.
- Mail ballots, provisional ballots, and close margins can make counts take days.
- Early results may shift as more votes are reported.
- Some states use runoffs when no one reaches the required share.
- Recounts happen in very close races, under rules that differ by state.
