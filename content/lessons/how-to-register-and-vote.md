Voting rules in the United States are set mostly by states, so the details change depending on where you live. This lesson covers the general steps. For your state's exact rules and deadlines, use [vote.gov](https://vote.gov), the official federal guide.

## Step 1: Check if you are eligible

In general, you must be a U.S. citizen and meet your state's age and residency rules. You can register before you turn 18 in many states, as long as you will be 18 by Election Day. [USA.gov](https://www.usa.gov/voter-registration) gives the basics.

## Step 2: Check your registration

Even if you think you registered before, check. People who move, change their name, or skip several elections can get dropped from voter lists. [Vote.gov](https://vote.gov) links to your state's official lookup tool.

## Step 3: Register

Most states let you register online. You can also register by mail or in person, such as at a motor vehicle office. Many states have a registration deadline before Election Day. Many allow same-day registration (see [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/same-day-voter-registration)). Find your state's deadline at [vote.gov](https://vote.gov/register).

## Step 4: Learn about ID

Some states ask for photo ID at the polls. Others accept things like a utility bill or a signature match. The [NCSL voter ID page](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/voter-id) gives a state-by-state summary, and [vote.gov](https://vote.gov) has official links. Check before you go so you are not surprised.

## Step 5: Pick how you will vote

Most states offer more than one way:

- **On Election Day:** Go to your assigned polling place. Lookup tools on vote.gov help you find it.
- **Early voting:** Many states open polling places days or weeks before Election Day.
- **Mail or absentee voting:** Some states mail ballots to everyone. Others require a reason or a request. Pay attention to deadlines, since some states need ballots received by Election Day and others count ballots postmarked by then.

## Step 6: Know your ballot

Look at a sample ballot before you vote. Many county or state election sites post one. You can also use [Vote411](https://www.vote411.org/), run by the League of Women Voters, to see what will be on your ballot. Bring notes if your state allows it.

## At the polls

Election workers are there to help. If your name is not on the list, you can usually ask for a provisional ballot, which is counted once officials confirm you are eligible. If you are in line when polls close, stay in line. You are allowed to vote.

## Primaries matter too

Before the general election, states hold primaries to choose each party's nominees. Rules differ on who can vote in which primary. Check [vote.gov](https://vote.gov) for dates.

## Key takeaways

- Rules come from states, so always check [vote.gov](https://vote.gov) for yours.
- Check your registration well before the deadline, even if you have registered before.
- Learn your state's ID rules ahead of time.
- You may be able to vote early, by mail, or in person on Election Day.
- Look at a sample ballot so you know what to expect.
