A governor is the top elected leader of a state. Think of the job as a state-level version of the president, with powers set by each state's own constitution.

## The main powers

Most governors can do these things:

- **Sign or veto bills.** When the state legislature passes a bill, the governor can sign it into law or veto it. In most states the legislature can override a veto with a larger vote, often two-thirds.
- **Propose a budget.** Governors usually offer a state budget plan covering schools, roads, health care, and public safety. The legislature has to approve it.
- **Run state agencies.** Governors appoint leaders of departments like transportation and health, and oversee thousands of state workers.
- **Command the National Guard.** The governor leads a state's National Guard unless the president takes federal control.
- **Respond to emergencies.** Governors can declare emergencies after storms, fires, or other disasters and ask for federal help.
- **Grant clemency.** Many governors can pardon people or reduce sentences, though some states use a board for that.
- **Fill some vacancies.** In many states, the governor appoints judges or replaces officials who leave office early.

For a plain overview, see [USA.gov's page on state governments](https://www.usa.gov/state-governments).

## Terms vary by state

Most governors serve four-year terms. New Hampshire and Vermont use two-year terms. Term limits also vary: some states cap governors at two terms, some allow only one in a row, and a few have no limit. The [National Governors Association](https://www.nga.org/governors/) lists every current governor and links to each state's governor's office.

Most governors are elected in midterm years. In 2026, 36 states held governor elections, according to [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Gubernatorial_elections,_2026).

## Not every state works the same

Some governors have strong powers, like a line-item veto, which lets them cancel parts of a budget bill. Others share power with other statewide elected officials, such as an attorney general or secretary of state, who are chosen separately by voters in many states.

This matters because a governor in one state may control things that another governor can't. Read about your own state before you vote.

## Governor vs. president

A governor can only act inside their state. They cannot make national laws, but they can shape how federal programs are run locally, like Medicaid. Governors also work with the state legislature, and the two can come from different parties, which sometimes slows things down.

## How to learn about your governor

Your state's official website, usually ending in .gov, has information about the governor's office. You can find it through [USA.gov's state directory](https://www.usa.gov/states-and-territories). For candidates, see our [research lesson](/learn/how-to-research-a-candidate).

## Key takeaways

- A governor leads a state's executive branch.
- Governors sign or veto bills, propose budgets, run agencies, and lead the National Guard.
- Most serve four-year terms, but New Hampshire and Vermont use two-year terms.
- Term limits and powers differ from state to state.
- Governors only have power within their own state.
