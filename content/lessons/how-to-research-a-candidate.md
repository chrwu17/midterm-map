You do not need to be an expert to make a smart choice. A few good habits are enough.

## Three kinds of sources

**Campaign websites and ads.** These show what a candidate wants you to know. They are good for learning priorities and promises. They are also marketing, so they will leave out weak spots.

**Voting records and public documents.** For current or past officeholders, what they did often matters more than what they say. Records show which bills they backed, how they voted, and what they sponsored. For Congress, use [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/), the official source for bills and votes.

**News coverage.** Reporters can check claims and give context. Look at several outlets, and note the difference between straight news reports and opinion pieces.

## Nonpartisan tools

- **[Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/)** is an encyclopedia of candidates, offices, and ballot measures.
- **[Vote411](https://www.vote411.org/)** from the League of Women Voters lets you enter your address and see your ballot, with candidate answers to questions.
- **[OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/)** tracks money in politics, including who donates to campaigns.
- **[Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/)** has bill texts, sponsors, and [roll-call votes](https://www.congress.gov/roll-call-votes).
- **[FEC.gov](https://www.fec.gov/)** is the official source for federal campaign finance reports.

## Spotting spin

Spin is when facts are framed to push you toward a conclusion. Watch for:

- **Vague words** like "fighting for you" with no details.
- **Cherry-picked numbers** with no comparison or time frame.
- **Out-of-context quotes** or clipped videos.
- **Big emotion, few facts.** If a post makes you furious fast, pause and check it.
- **Labels instead of details.** Words like "extreme" or "radical" don't tell you what someone did.
- **One vote, big claim.** A single vote on a long bill might include many unrelated parts.

## A simple routine

1. Look up your ballot on Vote411 or Ballotpedia.
2. Read each candidate's own website to learn their stated goals.
3. Check records if they've held office before.
4. Search for fact-checks and news from more than one outlet.
5. Look at who funds the campaign.
6. Compare candidates on the issues you care about most.

## It's fine to not know everything

Some offices, like local judges, get little coverage. You can skip a race if you're really not sure. A ballot with some blanks is still a valid ballot in most places. Check the rules with your [state or local election office](https://www.usa.gov/state-election-office).

## Key takeaways

- Compare what candidates say with what they have done.
- Use more than one source, and tell news apart from opinion.
- Ballotpedia, Vote411, OpenSecrets, and Congress.gov are good nonpartisan starting points.
- Be careful of vague claims, missing context, and posts built to make you angry.
- It's okay to skip a race you can't research.
